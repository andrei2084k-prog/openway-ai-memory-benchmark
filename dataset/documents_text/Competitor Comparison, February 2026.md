# Competitor Comparison, February 2026

Kitefold, Inc. | Internal document | February 16, 2026

Prepared by Maya Lindqvist, founder and CEO

Internal only. This document names competitors and discusses how we sell against them. It is for the team and must not be forwarded to prospects or customers, or pasted into anything they will read.

1. Why this document exists

Three companies come up most often when a prospect tells us they are looking at other options: Holoreef, Mirrorvale and Brassfinch. Each one wins a different kind of buyer, and each has a clear gap that we can use. I wrote this so that Paloma Reyes, Wes Okafor, Hannah Brook and the rest of the team are working from one shared picture instead of the memory of whoever took the last call.

The pricing and weakness facts below are the ones we are confident enough to repeat in a sales conversation as of February 16, 2026. Everything else in the positioning sections is our reading of their public material and of what prospects have told us. Treat it as a working view and correct it when a call tells you something different. Pricing in this market changes without notice, so check a competitor's public page before quoting any number to a prospect.

2. The three competitors at a glance

Holoreef. Enterprise plans from $60,000 a year. Sold through a sales-led process. Weakness: no self-serve plan, and a 6-week onboarding.

Mirrorvale. $99 a month for 30,000 views. Sold self-serve, on a low entry price. Weakness: no face effects and no white-label.

Brassfinch. $1,200 per published project, no subscription. Sold project by project. Weakness: no analytics beyond total views.

The three of them cover three different buying habits. Holoreef sells a large annual commitment, Mirrorvale sells a small monthly plan, and Brassfinch sells one project at a time. Kitefold sits across the middle of those, with plans that run from Kite up through Studio and Stage to Arena, and a product that a customer can start using without waiting for a long onboarding.

3. Holoreef

Positioning. Holoreef goes after large organizations that want a managed relationship. The pitch is a heavyweight platform with a vendor that does the work of rollout alongside the customer. It is a serious product aimed at buyers with procurement departments, and we should respect it.

Pricing. Enterprise plans from $60,000 a year. That is an annual commitment before the buyer has published anything, which is a hard conversation for a team that only wants to test an idea. It also means a buyer who is not sure about the value of the camera-based format takes on the whole risk up front.

Gap. Holoreef has no self-serve plan, and onboarding takes 6 weeks. For a marketing team with a launch coming up, six weeks of onboarding can be longer than the campaign window. A team that cannot try the product on its own has to sit through meetings and procurement steps before it sees anything running on a phone.

Where they are strong. A buyer who wants a vendor to carry most of the implementation work will find that appealing. Large, slow-moving organizations with formal vendor reviews may prefer a company that looks like their other enterprise suppliers. We should not pretend this segment does not exist. Our Arena plan is the answer for it, and Wes Okafor owns those conversations.

How to win against Holoreef

a. Lead with speed. A prospect can be building in our editor well inside the six weeks Holoreef needs just to finish onboarding. Offer to show a working experience early in the evaluation, not at the end of it.

b. Lead with the lack of a commitment barrier. Our entry plans let a team try the product before anyone signs for a year. When the prospect has an enterprise-sized need, we move them to Arena once the value is visible, so the conversation is about proven results and not a forecast.

c. Ask who will own the work after onboarding. If the answer is the customer's own team, they need to be comfortable in the tool, and that is easiest to judge by using ours.

d. Do not compete on the size of the annual contract. We will not win by implying that a smaller price means a smaller product. We win on time to first published experience and on a customer-friendly path from trial to scale.

4. Mirrorvale

Positioning. Mirrorvale is the low-friction option. A person can sign up, pay by card and publish something without speaking to anyone. It is the tool that small businesses and individual creators find first, and it often appears in a prospect's list simply because it is cheap and easy to find.

Pricing. $99 a month for 30,000 views. It is a simple, visible number and that helps them in short evaluations. The price is attractive until a campaign succeeds. A plan sized by views makes growth a cost conversation, and a buyer should do that arithmetic against their expected traffic before deciding.

Gap. Mirrorvale has no face effects and no white-label. Face effects are the feature that many consumer-facing campaigns ask for first, and our Facecast capability has been in public beta since September 2025. White-label matters to agencies that deliver experiences under their own name and do not want a third party's branding in front of their customers. Without it, an agency cannot present the work as entirely its own.

Where they are strong. Price, simplicity and a very quick start. For a one-off, basic experience on a tight budget, Mirrorvale can be a perfectly reasonable choice, and we should say so when it is true. We lose these deals if we sound as though we are selling something more complicated than the buyer needs.

How to win against Mirrorvale

a. Ask what the experience needs to do. If the answer includes a face effect, the comparison is over quickly, because they do not offer one. Show Facecast and be honest that it is still in public beta.

b. With agencies, ask whether they present the work under their own brand. Hannah Brook should raise white-label early, since it is a requirement that can disqualify a tool late in a project and cost the agency real embarrassment.

c. Move the conversation from the entry price to the full cost of a successful campaign. Ask for the expected number of views and compare plans on that basis.

d. Show what our analytics report, such as unique viewers and dwell time, so the buyer sees what they will be able to prove to their own stakeholders afterward.

5. Brassfinch

Positioning. Brassfinch sells projects, not software access. A buyer pays for each published experience and has no ongoing relationship with a plan. This suits a buyer who expects to publish one thing and move on, and it appeals to people who dislike subscriptions.

Pricing. $1,200 per published project, no subscription. The model is easy to explain and easy to approve, since each purchase is a defined amount tied to one deliverable. The cost for a team that publishes often grows with every project, and there is no point at which more usage makes the unit cost fall.

Gap. Brassfinch has no analytics beyond total views. A total view count says little about whether people engaged. It cannot separate a few people who opened an experience many times from many different people, and it says nothing about how long anyone stayed. Marketing teams are asked to justify spend, and total views alone make that hard.

Where they are strong. Predictability for a single project, and no commitment beyond it. A buyer with exactly one experience in mind, and no plan to make a second, may find the model sensible.

How to win against Brassfinch

a. Ask how many experiences the buyer expects to publish over the next planning period and let them work out what the per-project model means at that volume. Do not do the sum for them; they will trust it more if they do it themselves.

b. Ask how the buyer will report results. If the answer is anything more than a total view count, the analytics gap becomes the central issue. Show our reporting side by side with what they would get otherwise.

c. Point out that an experience can be edited and republished on a plan without paying for a new project each time. Confirm the exact behavior of the plan the prospect is considering before promising it.

6. Our own gaps

A comparison that only lists their weaknesses is not worth much. These are the places where we are exposed, and the team should be ready for them.

Facecast is in public beta. We can show it and we can sell the value of it, but we must describe it as a beta and not oversell stability. A buyer who needs a face effect for a hard launch deadline should be told plainly what beta means.

We are not the cheapest starting point. A buyer comparing only monthly sticker prices may find a lower entry number elsewhere. Our answer is value over the life of the campaign, and that argument has to be made with the buyer's own numbers.

We are not the vendor who does everything for the customer. A buyer who wants a managed rollout to be done for them can find that more naturally in an enterprise-style offer. Where that matters, we have to be clear about what our customer success team does and does not do, and Lena Marsh and Diego Ferreira should be brought into those conversations early.

Brand recognition. A larger competitor may be a safer-sounding name to a cautious committee. We counter this with working demonstrations and references, and only with a customer's written permission.

7. Summary: which competitor, which response

Prospect is a large organization with a formal procurement process and a long timeline: expect Holoreef. Lead with time to first published experience and with the lack of a year-long commitment up front. Wes Okafor leads.

Prospect is a small business or an agency comparing monthly prices: expect Mirrorvale. Lead with face effects and white-label, then with the cost of success at higher volume. Hannah Brook leads.

Prospect wants one experience and is wary of subscriptions: expect Brassfinch. Lead with reporting, and with what happens when the buyer wants a second and third experience.

8. Rules for talking about competitors

Keep it factual. Say what we know, say where it comes from, and stop. Do not guess at a competitor's roadmap, customers or finances, and do not repeat rumors.

Keep it out of anything external. This document, and the names in it, stay inside the company. Customer-facing writing, such as proposals, web copy, emails and webinar material, compares only in general terms and does not name them.

Keep it current. If a prospect shows you a competitor quote, a screenshot or a price that differs from this document, send it to Paloma Reyes so the next version is right. Ask the prospect's permission before sharing anything that came from their own documents, and share only what they agreed to.
